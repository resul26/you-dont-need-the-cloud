Chapter 14

Benchmarks — Stop Reading Charts You Don't Understand

A Reddit comment: "Dude I got 180 tokens/sec on my 4090."

Meaningless. A benchmark is only a benchmark if it records:

  • Exact model name and variant ("Qwen 3.6 27B Instruct" not "a 27B")
  • Weights format and quantization (EXL3 Q5, GGUF Q4_K_M, FP16, etc.)
  • Engine and version ("vLLM 0.4.2, CUDA 12.8")
  • Hardware (exact GPU, VRAM, driver version, RAM if relevant)
  • Input and output distribution (average prompt length? output length? mix?)
  • Concurrency (1 user? 10? 100? queue depth?)
  • Latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99, TTFT, TPOT)
  • Memory behavior under load (peak VRAM, KV cache use, fragmentation fail rate)
  • Cost per 1M tokens (for serving setups)

The golden rule: never compare engines on single-user tokens per second. Single-user speed is the number that everything downstream makes worse; it's marketing speed. Test your actual prompt and output distribution, with realistic concurrency, on the hardware you actually have.

And on leaderboards generally: leaderboards are for discovery. They are not a substitute for your own evals. The BF16 leaderboard number is not your Q4 24GB reality. Your workload is the benchmark that matters. Build a small eval set — 30 to 100 prompts from your real use — include expected answers, include latency and memory measurements, include failure categories, and evaluate candidates against that. Then pick.
