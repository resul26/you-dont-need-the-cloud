Chapter 1

What the Hell Is These Things Actually Doing?

Okay. Sit down. I'm going to explain a large language model in one paragraph and you're going to understand it, and then you're going to be annoyed that nobody explained it this way before.

An LLM does one thing. Over and over. It takes the text it has so far, and it guesses the next token. That's the whole thing. It doesn't "write an essay." It doesn't "know your codebase." It guesses the next token, appends that guess to what it has, and guesses again. A million-dollar machine doing the world's most expensive autocomplete, one token at a time.

Here's the loop, in order, because loops matter:

  • Your text becomes tokens (little number-chunks — more on those in a second).
  • Those tokens get fed into the model.
  • The model computes a score for every possible next token in its vocabulary.
  • Some policy picks one of them — could be the top-scoring one, could be a random draw from the top few.
  • That chosen token gets appended to the sequence.
  • Repeat until the model decides it's done, you decide it's done, or you run out of gas money (token limit).

That's it. That's inference. The fancy math people do the same thing in code:

Three words you'll see everywhere, so learn them now while they're cheap:

  • Logits — the raw scores, before anyone softens them into probabilities. Ugly, honest numbers.
  • Probabilities — those same scores after softmax makes them add up to 1. Friendly numbers.
  • Decoding — the policy that turns "a distribution of possible tokens" into "one specific token."

Now — why does this loop shape everything you buy? Because every piece of hardware pain you will ever feel lives in step 3 or step 6:

Try it — generation is sequential. This is all the model ever does.

No matter how big the machine, tokens come out one at a time. Faster hardware just means they come out less slowly. Every answer you have ever seen from an LLM arrived exactly like this.

  • Step 3 (computing scores for all previous tokens at the start) is called prefill. It's parallel. It's beefy. It's why a 20,000-token document takes twenty seconds before you see anything.
  • Step 6 (generating the answer one token at a time, repeatedly) is called decode. It's sequential. It's memory-bound. It's why your 24GB card is "fast" but your friend with the 128GB Mac says his is "useable for bigger stuff."

That's the entire book, basically. Prefill is compute-hungry. Decode is bandwidth-hungry. The KV cache (we'll get there, brace yourself) grows with every token. Everything else is a footnote.

Perception note: Long prefill = long pause before nothing happens. Slow decode = words appearing like it's 1997 dial-up. Both feel like the model is "slow," but they have totally different causes, and totally different fixes. People who don't separate them will waste months tuning the wrong thing. Don't be those people.
