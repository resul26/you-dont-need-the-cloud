Chapter 4

Why Your 24 Gig Card Cries at 32K Context

Quick refresher. Inference has two regimes:

PREFILL — reading your prompt, building the initial KV cache. This is compute-bound. GPUs love it. It parallelizes great. If you paste a 20,000-token document, prefill is the chunk of time where you're staring at a blank screen going "hello???"

DECODE — generating answer tokens one at a time. This is memory-bandwidth-bound, and it's sequential. Each new token needs the whole model's weights streamed through, plus the growing KV cache. This is the phase that determines whether your model "feels fast."

Why does this matter? Because it explains basically every hardware conversation you will ever have:

  • Short prompt, long answer? Decode is dominant. Memory bandwidth is your king. Buy for GB/s.
  • Long prompt, short answer? Prefill is dominant. Attention kernels and chunked prefill matter.
  • Lots of users hitting the same box? Scheduler quality becomes the thing. Continuous batching, prefix caching, fairness.
  • Long context? KV cache is the boss fight. PagedAttention, KV quantization, possibly offloading, possibly more VRAM.
  • MoE model? Expert routing dominates. All-to-all interconnect traffic.
  • Multi-GPU/multi-node? Interconnect — NVLink, RDMA, PCIe pipeline — becomes the ceiling.

I want to emphasize this because it's the single mistake that costs people the most time: decode speed tracks memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. And people spend weeks comparing FLOPs tables for models whose decode is bandwidth-bound. You will see this constantly. Feel free to pity them, but not so much that you join in.

Prefill vs Decode — same box, two completely different bottlenecks
2,048 tokens
1,000 GB/s
PREFILL — parallel. The GPU eats the whole prompt in batches. Compute-bound.
DECODE — one token at a time. Bandwidth-bound. This is the grid you actually wait on.

What controls how many tokens per second you actually see? In rough order of what matters most:

  • Memory bandwidth (dominates decode, especially single-user)
  • VRAM capacity (if you spill to CPU, speeds crater — sometimes 10–100×)
  • Quantization (less memory means less movement means more speed — up to a point)
  • Attention kernel quality (FlashAttention, SDPA, paged attention)
  • Batching / concurrency (throughput per user drops as you add users but total throughput goes up)
  • GPU FLOPs (matters for prefill and big batches; matters much less for single-user decode)
  • Prompt length & generated length (more tokens = more work, shocking nobody)
  • Runtime overhead (CUDA graphs, sampling, tokenizer, HTTP, LoRA switching, structured decoding — they each take a couple percent and then they unionize)

The most painful setup on the planet is the almost-fit setup: the model "fits" in VRAM but spills a layer or two of weights or a chunk of KV cache to system RAM over PCIe. It runs. It runs at 2 tokens per second. And you sit there going "the math said this should work" — the math did say it would fit, and it did not promise speed. Capacity and bandwidth are different things. Tattoo it.
