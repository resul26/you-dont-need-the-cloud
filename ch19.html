Chapter 19

RAG vs. Giant Prompts

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) means retrieve the relevant chunks, give the model only those. Simple idea. Hard implementation.

The pipeline has a dozen stages, each a failure point:

  • Ingestion — getting the documents in
  • Parsing — PDFs are a war crime against machines
  • Chunking — splitting into usable sizes
  • Embeddings — converting chunks into vectors
  • Vector index — the database
  • Retrieval — top-k lookup
  • Reranking — re-ordering retrieved candidates
  • Prompt construction
  • Answer generation
  • Grounding checks
  • Evaluation

The hard truth: most bad RAG systems aren't bad because of the LLM. They're bad because of chunking, retrieval, reranking, and evaluation.

Chunking is the silent killer. Fixed-size chunks with no overlap split sentences mid-thought and lose context. Semantic chunking, or hierarchical chunking with parent-document retrieval, works better — but there's no universal answer. You have to evaluate chunk size, overlap, and splitting strategy on your actual documents.

A good reranker rescues mediocre retrieval. No reranker fixes chunks that lost the answer during ingestion. So:

Long context is a complement to RAG, not a replacement.

Use RAG for large corpora. Use long context for the final selected evidence. A 1M-token context window isn't a knowledge base; it's a bracket. Stuffing a whole codebase into the prompt doesn't work reliably, and even when it fits, retrieve-first is almost always better.

Practical habits that help:

  • Put critical instructions near the beginning AND the end of your prompt.
  • Use section headers and delimiters.
  • Ask for citations tied to source chunks.
  • Compress conversational history.
  • Use summary memory instead of infinite chat history.
