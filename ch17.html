Chapter 17

Quantization — Quality Loss You Can Choose on Purpose

Quantization is the art of trading bits for memory (and sometimes speed). The 2026 cheat sheet:

  • FP16 / BF16 — the baseline for evaluations. Best quality when you can afford it.
  • Q8 / INT8 — near-lossless on a lot of tasks. Big, but useful.
  • Q6 / Q5 — strong middle ground. Good quality, moderate memory savings.
  • Q4 — the default consumer sweet spot for chat and document workflows.
  • Q3 / Q2 — for when you must fit a bigger model. Math, code, structured output, and tool use degrade first.

Weight quantization and KV cache quantization are different things:

  • Weight quant shrinks the model. You can quantize pretty aggressively with decent quality, especially at the larger sizes.
  • KV cache quant shrinks the live context. FP16/BF16 is the clean baseline. FP8/INT8 KV cache is a practical 2026 floor. Sub-8-bit KV cache is research-heavy, workload-sensitive, and should be tested before you trust it.

Where quantization failure shows up first, in roughly this order:

  • Math and multi-step reasoning
  • Code correctness
  • JSON / schema adherence and tool-call reliability
  • Subtle instruction following
  • Long-context retrieval

And the single best rule in here: a smaller model at higher precision can beat a bigger model crushed into too few bits. A 7B at Q6 often beats a 13B at Q2 on real reasoning tasks while using less memory and running faster. Don't worship parameter count. That's a leaderboard habit, not an engineering habit.

Pick your poison — every quant trades memory for quality. Watch the trade.

Quality bars are honest but illustrative — the real answer depends on your task. What’s a fact is the order: FP16 ≥ Q8 ≥ Q6 ≥ Q5 ≥ Q4 ≥ Q3 > Q2, and that math/code/JSON degrade first. The other fact is the orange line: Q4 is the default consumer sweet spot, and a smaller model at Q6 often beats a bigger one at Q2.
