Chapter 11

Inference Engines — You're Not Picking an Engine, Dummy

The big idea here, and I want it to land: you don't pick an engine first.

You pick a hardware strategy, a workload shape, and a serving model. The engine follows.

Pick hardware first. Figure out whether you have a 3090 desktop, a 2×4090 workstation, a Mac Studio, a DGX Spark, a laptop, a cluster. Then figure out whether you're a hobbyist, an agent-builder, an internal-tools person, or a public-facing serving operation. Then pick the engine.

What does an inference engine actually do? A lot more than people assume:

  • Loads weights
  • Runs the tokenizer
  • Runs the forward pass
  • Samples tokens per your decoding policy
  • Maintains the KV cache
  • Streams results
  • Handles batching, scheduling, prefix caching, paged attention
  • Applies quantization strategies
  • Manages tensor/pipeline/data/expert parallel execution
  • Exposes API surfaces (OpenAI-compatible, Anthropic Messages, gRPC, etc.)
  • Runs observability — metrics, tracing, logs

Ahmad's mental model: the engine is the traffic cop, memory manager, kernel dispatcher, scheduler, cache accountant, parallelism planner, and API surface — all at once. Sometimes it's also the deployment framework.

The best engine is the one that matches your memory hierarchy, your interconnect, your quantization format, your latency vs throughput targets, your architecture, and your operational maturity. Full stop.

Don’t pick an engine — answer three questions and the engine picks itself
